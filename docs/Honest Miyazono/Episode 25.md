A little after six that same evening.

Moonlight filtered through the lace curtains into Yukino Hasekura’s bedroom.

She had spent her time kneeling on the floor with her face buried in the bed, remembering Chiharu Miyazono and feeling her heart ache. When the tears came, she threw herself into her studies to distract herself from those feelings. Unable to find anywhere to put these unresolved, half-formed emotions, she had simply continued to drift through the hours, listlessly and out of habit.

Her phone, beside her pillow, began to vibrate and glow.

The light hurt her eyes, accustomed as they were to the darkness.

Yukino rubbed her swollen eyes, reached for the phone, and checked the screen.

The next instant, her face crumpled even more, and she rubbed at the corners of her eyes again.

A message had arrived. The sender was Chiharu Miyazono.

Actually, let's go to the summer festival after all.

The fireworks start at seven.

Let's watch them together.

The messages had arrived separately, accompanied by emoji. Yukino bit her lip.

She was happy to be invited. Honestly, part of her wanted to accept immediately, without a moment's hesitation.

But the instant she allowed herself to feel excited, Konatsu Sayama's words echoed through her mind.

Then tell me, can you yourself make Chiharu happy?

And the moment she thought about that question, Chiharu's hurt expression came to mind.

—【No, that's not—】.

That day, Chiharu had clearly been struggling through their conversation. If Yukino's romantic feelings were ever to be fulfilled, what awaited them would be a succession of those suffocating conversations.

Lies. The taste of words spoken by people trying to conceal something was, to Yukino, enough to make her vomit.

Naturally, someone as kind as Chiharu would try to interact with her without lying as much as possible.

Taking all that into account, there seemed to be no answer to the question of whether she could make Chiharu Miyazono happy other than: As long as I have this condition, it would be extraordinarily difficult.

That was why she ought to remain Chiharu's friend, keeping a comfortable distance between them. She was afraid that spending special moments together would make her waver in that resolve.

Yukino stared at the phone glowing in the darkness, agonising over how to reply.

That was when it happened.

Click.

She heard the faint sound of the front door unlocking.

Apparently, her mother was home. Since she wasn't making much noise, perhaps she hadn't brought a man back with her. Yukino was rubbing her eyes as she entertained that thought when, the very next moment—

Thud.

A sound and a vibration, as though someone had collapsed, travelled through the floor and reached the soles of Yukino's feet.

“Huh?”

Yukino let out a rather foolish-sounding cry, put down her phone, and cautiously left her room.

The flat had two bedrooms and a living-dining room. A corridor extended from the entrance, with two rooms on the right, and the living-dining room and kitchen at the far end. The bathroom and washroom were on the left.

In other words, when Yukino opened her bedroom door, the corridor lay directly in front of her.

And there, in the pitch-black corridor, she saw what appeared to be a woman, collapsed on the floor.

“Eek!”

As she let out a strangled cry, the emergency numbers for the police and ambulance flashed rapidly through her mind.

She was just about to hurry back to her room to retrieve her phone when her mother's hand seized her ankle.

“Waaah?!”

Yukino screamed involuntarily, but her mother said nothing, merely groaning.

Her concern for her mother naturally won out. She wanted to call an ambulance, but she couldn't get free.

As she wondered what to do, she finally noticed something was wrong.

An overpowering smell of alcohol assaulted her nostrils, so strong that it overwhelmed even the perfume she disliked. The sharp, pungent odour of booze. Now that she noticed it, even the hand gripping her ankle seemed hot to the touch.

Was this her mother's way of telling her not to call for help?

“…You've been drinking this early?”

Yukino spoke in a weary, half-lidded voice. At last, her mother managed to turn her indistinct groans into recognisable words.

“W-water… Get me some water… And carry me to my room.”

What an utterly hopeless mother. Yukino let out an exaggerated sigh.

“Not that one… The living room.”

Following her mother's instructions, Yukino supported the drunken, alcohol-reeking woman and hauled her into the living room.

Only the lace curtains were drawn. Naturally, the lights were off. The living-dining area contained a dining table with three chairs, a sofa beside a low table, and a flat-screen television that Yukino couldn't remember seeing switched on for quite some time. The room was pitch-black, lit only by the moonlight and the lights of the city outside.

It felt as though there ought to be a foul smell in this room, which had effectively become her mother's love hotel. Surprisingly, though, a closer look revealed that it was quite clean.

Yukino laid her mother on the sofa, then reluctantly helped her out of her jacket. She filled a random mug with water and brought it over. Her mother snatched it from her and gulped it down.

Since it was too dark to make out her face properly, Yukino picked up the remote to turn on the lights.

“Leave them off. The light makes my head hurt.”

Her mother said this dismissively, so Yukino obediently put the remote down with a “Right.”

She waited a few seconds, wondering whether there was anything else her mother needed. Seeing no sign of it, she began to turn away and head back to her room.

“Thanks.”

A voice barely distinguishable from a groan came flying after her. Yukino frowned and looked back suspiciously.

She knew her mother disliked talking to her. She also knew that her mother found Yukino's ability to understand people's emotions through synaesthesia creepy. And she knew that the words I love you were a lie.

Even so, this particular expression of gratitude had not been uttered carelessly. Confused, Yukino replied, “You're welcome.”

Then, quite unexpectedly, she remembered something Chiharu had said on the day they went to karaoke.

There are some things you can't know unless you try talking about them.

Before she knew it, Yukino had started a conversation with her mother of her own accord, something she hadn't done in a long time.

“I think… you should try not to drink so much.”

She ventured the words cautiously.

Her mother's breathing faltered slightly. Then came a sigh.

What's with that attitude?

Yukino felt frustrated and nearly turned back to her room. But, surprisingly, her mother responded.

“…How's school? Got any friends? You're home at this time of day. Are you managing all right?”

At least the ball Yukino had thrown hadn't simply been tossed aside. Her mother apparently had a ball of her own that she was willing to use for a little small talk.

Yukino sat on the floor beside the low table, hugging her knees. In the pitch darkness, neither could see the other's expression. They caught glimpses of each other's outlines and played a game of conversational catch.

“I… have friends. I'm doing well at school, too. I take time off on my days off.”\
“Ohhh… What about money? You got enough?”\
“O-of course I don't have enough. I have to prepare my own meals.”\
“Huh? Meals? That shouldn't cost that much. What are you spending it on?”\
“A-about 300 yen per meal? So, a thousand yen a day… I have other school supplies to pay for, too, and I don't get that many shifts at my part-time job.”

Worried that she might have said something strange, Yukino waited for a response.

“Hm?”

Apparently, they weren't quite on the same wavelength.

Her mother shifted into a supine position on the sofa, folded her arms, and ventured a theory.

“What, you mean you're actually doing a proper part-time job?”\
“I'd have to know what you mean by ‘proper’ to answer that… What did you think I was doing?”\
“Dating for money.”

“I'm working in a bookshop! I asked you to sign and stamp the employment contract!”

Yukino's voice cracked as she shouted back.

“Oh, right. Did you?”

Her mother groaned, sounding at least a little uncomfortable.

Yukino was left breathing hard, fighting back tears as she wondered why her own mother took so little interest in her.

“Well… You've got my face, after all. I figured you'd be making money.”\
“Dating for money is prostitution, you know. And if your daughter were doing something like that, shouldn't you stop her?”\
“Everyone was doing it when I was your age. Parents these days are too involved in their kids' lives.”\
“They aren't, though?”\
“Don't use me as your example. You've got cheeky while I wasn't looking…”\
“It wasn't just a little while.”

Yukino snapped back at everything like a rabid dog. Her mother, evidently fed up, took some banknotes out of her wallet as though trying to escape the argument.

“Here. Your dad's compensation and child support.”

Yukino swallowed all the things she wanted to say and began with, “Thank you.” She accepted the money gratefully in both hands. She could put it in her wallet later. For now, she slipped it into her pocket.

Watching Yukino's silhouette, her mother spoke in a languid, exasperated tone.

“You should tell me about these things sooner.”\
“Y-you should ask me yourself, as a parent. It's hard to talk when you're bringing men home… Besides, you don't love me, do you? I can tell, you know.”

Yukino retorted, remembering a lie from long ago.

Her mother laughed.

“Ku-hahaha.”

She didn't deny it.

She probably didn't love her. That much was surely not a lie.

And yet, she did things like ask how Yukino was getting on and give her money, at least fulfilling some of her parental obligations. Even as her own daughter, Yukino couldn't understand what her mother was thinking. Her mother was supposed to dislike her.

Her suspicion gradually began to turn into curiosity.

Come to think of it, she realised, she knew absolutely nothing about her mother.

She wanted to ask her something, but what would be a good question?

When she thought about it, the first thing that came to mind was romance. She could hardly call her own feelings a pure love, but perhaps there was something she could learn from her mother, who was immersed in a kind of sexual love utterly different from her own.

“Mum, you're always going out with different people… Do you have no intention of marrying anyone?”

Her question was met with several seconds of silence.

Eventually, her mother replied in a drawn-out voice.

“No. I don't. There's no benefit to it. I don't want to love someone. I want someone to love me. I don't want to do things for someone else. I always want to be the one who receives.”

What an extraordinary mother. What an extraordinary woman.

Yukino stared at her, dumbfounded.

“You've got my face, too, so you must be popular, right?”

Faced with this next question, Yukino struggled to produce a reply, her mouth still hanging open in disbelief.

“I do get confessed to sometimes, but… with this condition of mine…”\
“Ah… If you don't tell them, you'll have a hard time. If you do, they'll have a hard time. You're just unlucky, aren't you?”

That was true, but how could she say something so bluntly to her own daughter?

Yukino gave her a suspicious, half-lidded stare in the darkness.

But her mother continued.

“Well, then. Don't choose a man who won't make you happy.”

Yukino mulled over those words for a moment, then decided to correct her.

“…I think I like women.”\
“Same thing, same thing. Either way. You should always be the one who gets made happy.”

Her mother's advice seemed to suggest that Yukino's sexual orientation was of no importance.

Clashing with it in Yukino's mind was Konatsu's question: Can you make Chiharu happy?

Konatsu's words won out. Whatever the case, she didn't think it was right to be the only one who received happiness.

“Shouldn't it work both ways? If you can't make the other person happy, then… you shouldn't be in a relationship…”\
“Huh? Who cares as long as you're happy? You should live a little more selfishly.”\
“N-no! That's obviously wrong!”\
“Why?”\
“Because it's not sincere. I have a very close example of what not to do.”\
“Haaah… Are you really my daughter?”\
“Please try doing a little more parenting before saying things like that.”\
“Fuhahaha.”

Her mother had no retort. She let out an amused breath, and perhaps the alcohol was finally beginning to wear off. With a sigh, she rested an arm across her forehead and stared up at the ceiling.

Still hugging her knees, Yukino wondered when she had last exchanged so many words with her mother.

Back when they had been going to hospitals because of her condition, had they used to have conversations this trivial?

“Mum, I always thought you didn't like me very much.”

Yukino murmured the words.

After several seconds of silence, her mother answered calmly, giving her honest opinion.

“Well, I don't like talking to someone who can see right through my feelings. It's creepy.”\
“Ugh!”

Yukino's face twisted involuntarily. It was hard to believe that was genuinely how her mother felt.

Her emotions were easy to read, too. There was not even the slightest sweetness of affection in her voice. It was simply flavourless and scentless.

“In the first place, I didn't love your dad, either. And I don't love you.”\
“So that's how it is… Do people normally say that to their daughter's face?”\
“And while we're at it, I don't want to make you happy, either.”

Yukino reflected that there was no point asking any further. But then her mother added one more thing.

“Still, I do think you should go ahead and be happy on your own.”

She didn't want to pay the cost. She didn't want to devote any of her life's resources to it.

If her mother considered love to be making someone happy by paying those costs and committing those resources, then her words up to this point were certainly consistent.

And yet… while she wasn't willing to invest those resources, she did at least wish for Yukino to be happy.

To her, that was not love. It was simply a feeling.

Most people would probably call her attitude child neglect and abuse. And they would be right.

But Yukino couldn't bring herself to hate that part of her mother. As she murmured, “I see,” her lips softened ever so slightly, sensing the affection that had, in its own way, surely been there all along.

In the end, her mother was someone who wanted to be loved by others without loving anyone herself.

Perhaps she shouldn't accept that.

But Yukino smiled with her usual half-lidded expression and decided, for the moment, to love her anyway.

“By the way, the way you drank today worries me. Please don't do that so often.”

Her mother answered with a low, groaning breath of silence.

It seemed to have struck a nerve, so Yukino continued.

“Also, I don't really like you frequently bringing men home. Please stop doing that, too.”

This was a selfish request from a daughter, but, surprisingly, her mother didn't reject it.

Eventually, she rolled over towards the back of the sofa and groaned listlessly.

“I'll see what I can do.”

After a while, still lying in that position, her mother spoke again.

“You should eat a little more, too. You're too thin.”\
“B-but whose fault is that…? I don't eat much because I don't have enough money. And I throw up, too.”\
“If you don't have enough money, come and ask me for some.”\
“You're never home, and when you are, you're bringing men back!”\
“Your phone.”\
“Grrr… B-but normally, parents are supposed to take an interest themselves, aren't they?”

It was true that her mother had bought her a phone, and Yukino could have used it to ask for money.

But for some reason, she had held on to the fixed idea that she ought to face her mother and ask her verbally.

Yukino reached for her phone, then remembered that she had left it in her bedroom.

After a while, her mother fell silent again. She rolled onto her back and began breathing regularly.

Yukino was just about to stand up and return to her room without waking her when her mother murmured:

“You've got someone you like, haven't you?”

Yukino nearly felt her heart leap out of her mouth. She widened her eyes, clapped a hand over her lips, and frantically retraced their conversation.

Had she said anything that might have given it away?

As Yukino panicked, her mother continued.

“You've changed a little. And you said you liked women because you've fallen for one, right?”

It was true that nearly ninety per cent of people were heterosexual, and many who had never questioned their own orientation would probably assume that they were, too.

She's surprisingly sharp, Yukino thought, rather rudely.

Her mother went on.

“Tell me when you go on a date. I'll teach you how to get the other person to pay.”\
“From the way you put it, I thought you were going to give me money for the date. I'm not asking you to teach me that.”

Yukino retorted instinctively.

“Besides…”

She remembered her current relationship with Chiharu.

“Because of my synaesthesia… that person finds it very difficult to talk to me. I don't think she can be happy when she's with me. I don't think I should fall in love with someone if I can't make them happy.”

Her mother fell silent, as though thinking.

The silence went on so long that Yukino began to wonder whether she had fallen asleep. Then, without ceremony, her mother tossed back the ball Yukino had thrown.

“…Then tell me this. If that girl made you unhappy, would you stop loving her?”

The question was delivered in such a casual tone, but Yukino thought it had struck right at the heart of the matter.

She instinctively tried to argue back, but gradually her response faltered, until she finally admitted it.

“That… I wouldn't.”\
“Hmph.”

Her mother snorted and crossed her legs, still lying on the sofa.

“Don't go around acting all high and mighty about sincerity and responsibility, you stupid kid in your late teens. What a pain. It's not a coin. It's not as though if you get one side, the other side can't come up. Happiness and unhappiness are things you experience together, aren't they? Didn't you say it yourself? People's emotions have complicated flavours.”

Her condition made it impossible to avoid one of two things: either preventing people from lying, or allowing them to lie and keeping quiet about it. One way or another, someone would end up feeling miserable.

That much was undoubtedly true. But it did not necessarily mean that the condition itself would make both people miserable for the rest of their lives.

Those things were not black and white. They could coexist.

“For the first time in a long while, you looked like my mother.”

Yukino said this with a perfectly serious expression, as though scales had fallen from her eyes.

It was an extraordinarily rude thing to say.

But her mother showed no sign of taking offence. Instead, she spat out, with an amused laugh:

“You stupid girl.”

Then she said, “I'm going to sleep,” and rolled over towards the back of the sofa again.

Yukino watched her indistinct silhouette, waiting until her breathing settled into a steady rhythm.

“Good night.”
