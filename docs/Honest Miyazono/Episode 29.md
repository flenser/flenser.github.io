As Chiharu and the mother of the lost child made their way towards the lost children’s centre, they spotted a festival staff member wearing a fluorescent vest. Chiharu, drenched in sweat and wearing an expression of fierce urgency, hurried over and questioned them. Taken aback by her intensity, the staff member nevertheless asked for a description of the missing girl, checked over their radio, and then gave them a cheerful thumbs-up.

The mother’s face, upon hearing the good news, looked ready to burst into tears at any moment.

The two of them then hurried towards the lost children’s centre, which had become their meeting point, dragging their aching feet along the way.

She had come wearing high heels, yet she must have run the whole time without stopping to look around. Her feet looked terribly sore. And yet she was still hurrying along, eager to see her daughter. Mothers really are remarkable.

“Thank you so much… Truly, thank you.”

The woman offered her words of gratitude, looking at Chiharu, who was perhaps even more drenched in sweat than she was.

She was probably in her late twenties or early thirties. Young, certainly, but considerably older than Chiharu. She had maintained such polite speech right to the end. Feeling a little self-conscious at the formality between them, Chiharu straightened up and returned her smile.

“It’s such a special day, after all. Wouldn’t you want to bring it to a special end?”

As Chiharu smiled, remembering the girl she had hurt, the woman replied, “Yes,” and wiped away her tears.

“But why would you… go to such lengths for us? Are you, um, from around here?”

The woman looked at Chiharu as though unsure how to accept such kindness.

Chiharu folded her arms, wearing an uncertain expression, and accepted the question at face value.

“I’m not one of the staff or anything, but… well, I suppose I can see how it might seem suspicious.”\
“Oh, no! I didn’t mean to be rude! It’s just that, if I imagine myself in the same situation, I wonder whether I could do the same.”

Chiharu certainly did not think everyone would do it, nor would she claim that just anyone could.

But she did not think it was some extraordinary act of goodness, either, so she raised a finger.

“Have you heard of the theories of innate human goodness and innate human evil? I mean the commonly misunderstood versions.”\
“Oh, yes. The idea that people are born either good or bad, right?”\
“That’s it, that’s it. This isn’t some spiritual thing, but… I believe in the innate goodness of people. Though I suppose that includes a fair bit of wishful thinking, too. I want it to be true, at any rate. What I mean is…”

Rather than asking, ‘Why would someone go to such lengths?’ Chiharu thought the question was always, ‘Why wouldn't they?’

If someone was out enjoying themselves with friends, they might think, ‘I'm too busy to get involved with other people.’ If they disliked people, they might think, ‘It won't affect me if the child isn't found.’

In other words, she wanted to believe that, at their core, everyone was good, herself included.

“It wasn’t that I had a reason to do something. I just didn’t have a reason not to.”

It sounded rather cool when put that way. Chiharu declared it with a flourish, and the woman stared at her blankly. Then, at last, she finally managed a small smile.

“Ah, there it is! We're almost there!”

Chiharu pointed towards a large sign.

The words on it read LOST CHILDREN'S CENTRE. The two of them hurried towards it.

The Yōka Festival occupied the main road all the way from the station to the city hall, with additional sections branching off into the surrounding streets. The lost children’s centre was located just west of an intersection a little closer to the station than the midpoint of the main thoroughfare.

In an open space without any food stalls, a pristine white temporary tent had been erected. Beneath it stood several tables with warm-coloured lights, the sort that put people at ease, and a number of folding chairs.

Before long, the woman seemed to forget all about her aching feet and broke into a run, calling out her daughter's name.

“Nanako! Nanako, where are you?!”

An older female staff member standing some distance away waved a glowing traffic baton to guide them over.

And beside her stood a little girl who looked almost exactly like the woman, just as Chiharu had been told.

The girl had been standing expressionlessly under the protection of the adults around her. When she noticed her mother, she murmured, “Mummy,” and ran towards her, her face still blank.

But then, perhaps seeing the tears threatening to spill from her mother's eyes, her expression began to crumple as she ran. Tears welled up around her eyes.

“Mummyyy…”

Her voice came out muffled and tearful.

The woman burst into tears, bent down to meet her daughter's gaze, and wrapped her in her arms.

She held her so tightly, so very tightly, that Chiharu almost worried the girl might not be able to breathe.

Everyone involved in the incident watched them with gentle expressions. Some of the staff who had apparently gone out of their way to help even looked rather proud, rubbing beneath their noses.

And then, the two girls in their late teens who had been watching from a distance met each other's eyes.

When they first caught sight of one another, they had been surprised. And yet, contradictory as it might seem, they had not been all that surprised, either.

More than anything, they had both found themselves thinking that the other would surely have acted in the same situation.

Remembering how embarrassed she had been after crying on the phone just a little while ago, Yukino fiddled with her fringe. Chiharu read the gesture and couldn't help returning a teasing smile.

Still, neither of them looked particularly suited to a tender, bittersweet reunion. They were both drenched in sweat from head to toe, making it quite clear how hard they had worked.

Eventually, the mother, now calmer, gently scolded her daughter for wandering off on her own.

When the girl explained, half in tears, what she had wanted to do, her mother hugged her again, crying as she did so.

And in about three minutes, everything was settled. Mother and daughter held hands so as not to lose each other again, and made sure to thank everyone who had helped them. The mother thanked Chiharu, then Yukino.

The daughter, meanwhile, stared intently at Yukino.

“Thank you, big sister.”

The girl gave her a little bow, as children do. Yukino smiled and waved a hand.

“Don't mention it.”

When the girl raised her head, she was wearing a soft, happy smile. It was the first time Yukino had seen her smile like that.

And the moment she saw it, Yukino felt all the strength drain from her body.

A sense of accomplishment. That was certainly part of it, but the feeling that overwhelmed her more than anything was one of understanding.

Yes… understanding.

“…Oh. I see.”

Yukino murmured the words under her breath. Chiharu, who had somehow appeared beside her, looked at her curiously.

Hasekura Yukino had always believed that her condition, which forcibly exposed lies, created friction, and that this friction would make the friends and lovers who became involved with her unhappy.

That was why her own desire to be with someone had seemed selfish: she was turning a blind eye to the fact that she would make the other person unhappy.

And she had finally acknowledged that this was something neither she nor Chiharu could deny.

Even so, knowing the risks, Yukino had made her selfish request, trusting that Chiharu would accept her as she was. And Chiharu had shown her a way of thinking that placed the two of them together at its centre. Rather than making each other happy or being made happy by the other, they would become happy together.

There was just one crucial thing Yukino had overlooked. And now, she had finally realised it.

This entire incident had taken place without her condition coming into play at all. She was simply frail, physically weak, and yet she had done her best to carry that little girl on her back and bring her here.

And now, she had brought about a smile.

Which meant…

*

“My condition might make someone unhappy, but perhaps I myself can make people happy.”

*

It felt as though it had taken her an awfully long time to realise something so simple.

Chiharu, listening beside her, pursed her lips as though trying to hide her feelings. But she couldn't quite hold back the tears gathering at the corners of her eyes.

“That's right,” she murmured, nodding.

“Yeah. That's right.”

Thinking back, it had always been that way. Chiharu Miyazono had initially felt sympathy for Hasekura Yukino because of her condition. But what had drawn her to Yukino had always been the way she worked so hard for other people.

Helping out when things were busy. Exposing the hidden camera. Finding things that had been concealed. Drawing someone out of an outing they hadn't wanted to attend.

Perhaps her condition had helped make those things possible, but the person who had chosen to act was Hasekura Yukino herself.

“Oh, we're going to eat cotton candy together.”

The girl left Yukino with those final words, and her mother gave a slightly bashful smile and nodded.

Yukino wore a radiant expression, as though a weight had finally been lifted from her shoulders. And yet, there was still a hint of her usual charm in that open, unguarded smile as she watched the close-knit mother and daughter with a touch of envy and waved goodbye.

“It's dangerous to walk while you're eating, so make sure you enjoy it together, nice and slowly.”

And so she watched the mother and daughter walk away, still holding hands, until their figures disappeared into the crowd.

The very next moment…

The clock struck seven.

A whistling sound rang through the air. Almost everyone at the festival turned towards it.

Against the pitch-black sky, decorated by the stars and moon, lanterns hung in the foreground. A silver streak shot vertically upwards.

Then came an explosive crack.

A magnificent firework blossomed across the night sky.

The crowd cheered as sparks scattered in every direction. Some people stopped to raise their phones and take pictures.

The scent of gunpowder mingled with the aromas of the food stalls, carried along on the summer breeze.

It was a shame that this wasn't exactly the best place to watch from. The staff who had worked so hard for others, and the two girls themselves, had hardly been in a position to secure a good spot. A few voices expressed mild regret.

But the two of them had exchanged messages saying they would be late, knowing full well that this might happen. And now, as the fireworks painted their smiles in brilliant, multicoloured gradients, they turned to look at one another.

“The fireworks have already started.”\
“Yeah.”
