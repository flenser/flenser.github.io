After school, as the students streamed out of the classroom in every direction, Chiharu came over to Yukino’s desk.

With her bag slung over her shoulder, Chiharu wore an expression that seemed almost suffocating. It was practically saying, Shall we go home together? Yukino, who had been sitting with her head bowed and her own face twisted in troubled thought, managed to recover a faint smile and nodded.

They said nothing as they left the classroom, made their way down to the entrance hall, changed their shoes and stepped outside.

Were their romantic feelings genuine? Or was it sympathy?

Should they enter a relationship when they knew they might not be able to make the other person happy?

Such questions sent their thoughts spiralling into a complicated maze. Unable to find any answers, the two of them walked beneath the oppressively lukewarm sunlight of the late afternoon. Gradually, like ice melting, their lips loosened. They both let out a sigh and began to speak at the same time. Their breaths overlapped, and when they realised it, they exchanged glances, each yielding the floor to the other.

After a moment's hesitation, Chiharu furrowed her brow, though a smile played around her lips, and began.

“So... I suppose you've already heard from Konatsu.”\
“Yes, I have. I heard she asked you to go out with her. And that you're still considering it.”\
“Ah, no...” Chiharu quickly interrupted.

Perhaps it was the July heat. Sweat had begun to gather on her forehead.

“It's not that I'm still considering it, exactly. Konatsu pointed out quite a few things to me. It felt a bit like a romantic audit, but there were things she said that I thought were fair. So, how should I put it...? For the time being, I think I want to properly face up to what she pointed out.”

Chiharu's words were unusually hesitant. Yukino felt a sharp little pain in her chest.

There was no lie in what Chiharu had said, but the truth she had chosen to share was that she intended to take Konatsu's confession seriously. Wasn't Chiharu supposed to have feelings for her? Why wouldn't she simply turn Konatsu down? Yukino hated herself for allowing such selfish questions to surface. Her face twisted as she lowered her head, biting her lip before somehow managing to summon a smile.

I'll make Chiharu happy. I'm confident I can. What about you?

Konatsu's words suddenly came back to her.

Oh, I hate this, Yukino thought.

She hated herself for not having been able to answer that question immediately, and for still being unable to answer it now. And she hated herself for letting that uncertainty prevent her from expressing the simple desire to be the one Chiharu looked at.

She hadn't even confessed yet. She had never doubted that things would end in the happy ending they seemed destined to reach.

If this was how things stood, perhaps this was the moment she should tell Chiharu how she felt.

But the words wouldn't come. A declaration of love made while knowing you might be incapable of making the other person happy was tantamount to saying, Go to hell. To ignore that and simply blurt out her feelings would be nothing but self-deception.

“...Chiharu-san's wishes come first. So please, take all the time you need.”

Yukino wore a fixed smile, making her impartial and fair-minded position clear. A trace of relief appeared on Chiharu's face.

But what was that faint emotion welling up behind her eyes? Loneliness, perhaps?

Once, Yukino Hasekura had taken Chiharu by the hand and led her away from an outing with classmates she hadn't really wanted to spend time with. Now, when it came to Chiharu's struggle over whether to abandon her romantic feelings, Yukino had offered nothing but a neutral, indifferent remark.

Chiharu crumpled the fabric over her chest in her fist, then straightened her tie as though to cover up the gesture.

She put on a convincing smile and nodded.

“Yeah.”

They passed through the school gates and reached the two-lane road leading towards the nearest station. A lukewarm summer breeze blew past them.

*

From Konatsu Sayama's perspective, people were liars.

In particular, she felt that many people lied to themselves. You could call it self-deception.

For example, when Konatsu was in primary school, there had been an honour student sitting in the desk in front of hers. One day, the girl was working on a group assignment with a boy who had a forceful personality. The boy never paid attention in class, and as far as Konatsu could observe, the girl was effectively doing all the work herself. It was a demanding task, spread across several lessons, and each time they worked on it, the girl asked the boy to help. But it was like talking to a brick wall.

Then, one day, the girl's patience finally ran out. She loudly berated the boy.

Well said, Konatsu had thought from her seat behind them, impressed.

But when the homeroom teacher intervened, the first thing they pointed out was that the girl's choice of words had been inappropriate. The boy received the same half-hearted warning he always got, and that was the end of it.

The girl had been stunned. Konatsu felt as though she were witnessing something unbelievable.

I suppose they'd worked it out rationally.

It was cheaper to reprimand a serious, obedient girl and make her accept the situation than to spend the effort trying to get an uncooperative boy to do his share. The cost of the former was lower.

Unable to accept that, Konatsu raised her voice in protest on behalf of the girl, who had begun to cry. But the teacher insisted, “It's the teacher's job to give them a warning. You can't just take it upon yourself to do that.” Young as she was, Konatsu couldn't think of a way to argue back.

It was self-deception.

When Konatsu was in secondary school, there had been bullying in her class. A group of assertive girls ignored one of their more timid classmates, teased her and made fun of her. They were a cunning group, always careful to stay just within the limits of what they could get away with, even if their behaviour came to light.

Konatsu did not help the girl.

She thought that even if she did everything a student could reasonably do, she wouldn't be able to change anything.

But neither did she join in with the bullying.

All she did was report every incident to their homeroom teacher in meticulous detail, urging the teacher to deal with it.

And then, while the bullying continued, the day finally came.

The ringleader of the group was coming down the stairs when she tapped the girl's shoulder, intending to startle her. The girl fell down the stairs. Her right arm was broken, and she was bleeding from the head. Whatever the intention might have been, the circumstances made it unmistakable: she had been pushed down the stairs. According to one of the other group members' later testimony, the ringleader, who had been wearing an unpleasantly smug grin, went deathly pale.

What followed was a succession of weak, dishonest people trying to justify themselves.

The bullying group described their actions as “just fooling around”.

The homeroom teacher insisted they “hadn't known about the bullying” and had “thought the girls were friends”.

Konatsu regretted what she had done. She had decided that resolving bullying was the teacher's responsibility, and that protecting herself was her own responsibility. She had never tried to protect the girl to the fullest extent of what she could personally do.

So, as the least she could do to make amends, she testified publicly that she had reported the bullying to the homeroom teacher.

Naturally, the teacher denied it. But another teacher, who knew that Konatsu had repeatedly sought them out, corroborated her account. The school then concluded that “one teacher had turned a blind eye to bullying”.

At that point, the bullying group realised their own position was in danger. They claimed, “We were only teasing her. It was one of the ringleaders who pushed her.” The girl who had actually pushed the victim down the stairs transferred to another school.

People were evil. Human beings were fundamentally neither righteous nor strong. They were weak and fragile, and so they banded together and lied.

—That was why meeting Miyazono Chiharu in high school had felt like being struck by spring thunder.

*

One day in her first year of high school, Konatsu was assigned to a group project with five classmates.

The subject was civics, and the topic concerned international society.

There were six members in total: three girls and three boys. A terrible combination.

Two of the boys spent the entire time chatting and slacking off. One of the girls was desperately trying to catch the attention of one of them. The remaining girl used the excuse that they should divide the work according to their respective strengths to dump all the tedious tasks on Konatsu. In practice, Konatsu ended up doing the entire assignment herself, from beginning to end.

She was aware that her spirit had been worn down by her experiences in secondary school. Perhaps she could even have called it a distrust of people. That was why she thought it would be easier simply not to expect anyone to cooperate. She couldn't even be bothered to expend the energy needed to complain, so she quietly got on with the work.

The group project was scheduled to take four hours to complete. They were halfway through when the end-of-class bell rang. Konatsu put the papers away in her desk and left the classroom. Someone tapped her on the shoulder.

“Hey, why are you doing all the work yourself?”

It was Miyazono Chiharu, the class representative, with whom Konatsu had barely interacted at the time.

Konatsu remembered giving her a suspicious look and rattling off a few excuses, trying to brush her off. She had been fed up with the idea that Chiharu was simply trying to earn points for herself as class representative, or that this was just another bit of meddling.

But Chiharu wasn't fooled by such flimsy words. She replied with something so cold that it made Konatsu's chest ache. Irritated, Konatsu told her the truth. The others had no intention of doing the work. They were uncooperative, and all they did was chat, getting in the way. It was easier and faster to do it herself.

There was no point telling the teacher. They wouldn't do their job anyway.

There was no point telling the group members either. She was in the minority.

Everyone lived to make things easier for themselves. They worked hard to justify their own laziness.

Most people were scum, stupid, incompetent...

Konatsu stated all this matter-of-factly, concluding that this was simply how things were.

But then—

“That's certainly true.”

Miyazono Chiharu nodded with complete seriousness.

Perhaps that was the moment Konatsu fell for her.

Before she knew it, Chiharu was already taking action on her behalf. First, she took Konatsu around the school during a break, then contrived to run into their civics teacher by chance. Chiharu put on the very sort of superficially pleasant, insincere smile that Konatsu hated, and explained the situation, mixing in a few lies along the way.

The teacher's response was little different from what Konatsu had experienced in secondary school: a vague promise to give the others a formal warning, without addressing the details.

Konatsu was exasperated. She let out a quiet sigh of disappointment.

That was where she and Chiharu differed.

After hearing the teacher's response, Chiharu put on a playful expression and asked a question that was almost threatening.

If everyone receives the same assessment based solely on the finished work, doesn't that mean their grades are determined by the luck of their group members, regardless of their own effort? Is persuasion itself supposed to be a learning experience? But shouldn't responsibility come with authority? We have no authority to make our group members do as they're told. Isn't the purpose of social education to teach us how to deal with unreasonable situations, rather than simply to teach us that unreasonable situations exist?

Konatsu couldn't help admiring how effortlessly Chiharu could reel off such a long argument.

In the end, the teacher verbally promised to award marks separately according to each student's classroom performance up to that point. They also promised to speak to the other students before the next lesson, so that the finished work would be of a higher standard. And Chiharu even made sure to get a clear commitment about what would happen if the teacher broke that promise.

She was merciless.

As a result, the other group members panicked and began to take the initiative, albeit reluctantly and grudgingly. With more people contributing, the quality of the work improved noticeably. On top of that, Konatsu was promised additional marks for the effort she had already put in.

In other words, the outcome for Konatsu Sayama could hardly have been better.

Furthermore, the teacher had been made to fulfil their responsibilities, and the other group members had been given a reason to fulfil theirs. Their grades would clearly improve compared with what they would have received had they continued slacking off. No one had lost anything. Thanks to Miyazono Chiharu, everything had simply become better than it was before.

If anyone had lost out, it was perhaps Miyazono Chiharu herself, who had gone out of her way to act despite having nothing to gain.

But when Konatsu questioned her about it, Chiharu simply laughed and said:

“Come on, isn't it annoying? When people who work hard end up getting the short end of the stick?”

That was how the two of them became friends, and the moment Konatsu fell in love with Chiharu.

—From Konatsu Sayama's perspective, people were liars. Human beings were fundamentally neither righteous nor strong. They were weak and fragile, and so they banded together and lied.

That perception had not changed, either then or now.

But she had added one qualification: Not everyone is like that.

There were people who might not be righteous, but who still strove to do what was right. People who might be weak, but tried to be strong for someone else's sake. People who might be fragile, but could support one another precisely because of it. People who might band together, but did so out of kindness rather than weakness.

There were people who might be liars, but who remained honest when it came to justice.

Perhaps, if Konatsu could become someone like that, then even when she encountered people she had failed to protect in the past, she might be able to do something for them.

“Apparently the annual Yōka Festival is happening next Saturday. Hey, why don't we all go together?”

Yamazaki's cheerful voice came from the front of the classroom, and Yukino found herself looking up.

It was lunchtime, the day after Konatsu had declared war on her. Yukino had just picked up the lunch box Chiharu routinely gave her, ready to go and eat, when she heard that invitation.

She had arranged to have lunch with Chiharu today, but the very Chiharu in question was caught up in Yamazaki's conversation, leaving Yukino unable to go anywhere. Besides, she and Chiharu had promised to go to the festival together. Their relationship had been a little awkward lately, but that didn't mean Chiharu would go back on her promise—

“Ah, sorry. I don't think I can. Count me out.”

—It turned out to be needless worry.

Chiharu declined calmly and stood up from her seat with her lunch box, without waiting for a response.

Yamazaki, who knew her well, simply laughed and accepted it with an exasperated, “Good grief.”

But one of the boys in the group, who perhaps had a crush on Chiharu, pressed the matter a little further.

His name was Ota, if Yukino remembered correctly. He was a tall member of the football club, popular with the girls.

“Aw, come on. You're always like this. You're no fun. Let's all go out together for once.”\
“I already have plans.”\
“Then bring that person along. Is it a guy or a girl?”

As Ota continued to press her, Chiharu let out an unmistakably exasperated sigh. Yamazaki stepped in to admonish him.

“Ota.”

But he seemed to be the sort of person who never doubted that everyone liked him. Had he lived in a different era, he might well have been the sort to enthusiastically champion the geocentric model of the universe. He kept following Chiharu around, oblivious, trying to persuade her.

Chiharu ignored him and came over to Yukino's desk, a faint smile appearing through her weariness.

“Shall we go and eat, Yukino?”

Must be hard being so popular, Yukino thought, as though it had nothing to do with her.

“Ah, y-yes! Let's go.”

Yukino glanced at Ota as she got up from her chair. He had been ignored, but he was still trying to find a way to keep Chiharu there. In a panic, he seized on a compliment.

“Ah... Is Hasekura-chan the person you already have plans with? Come to think of it, you two have been eating together a lot lately. I didn't know you were connected. Wow, Miyazono, you're so kind! Hasekura-chan's usually on her own, after all.”

Yukino gave an uncertain smile, looking from Chiharu to Ota and back again, repeating an awkward little gesture that was somewhere between a glance and a bow.

Ota evidently still wanted to stop Chiharu from leaving. With a sheen of sweat on his face, he continued:

“...You're only keeping her company because you feel sorry for her.”

Apparently, the careless step he had just taken had brought his foot down on a tiger's tail.

Chiharu stopped.

Then she turned around.

The expression on her face seemed to send ripples of silence spreading through the classroom, like a stone dropped into still water.

“What did you say?”

Chiharu's voice was so cold and sharp that it sent a shiver down the skin. Her usual friendly smile had vanished completely. The emotion on her face could only be described as hostility.

Ota suddenly understood that if he said the wrong thing, she would publicly tear him apart and humiliate him. He also realised that whatever sense of camaraderie he had built up with his classmates had crumbled and vanished in an instant.

He immediately grasped the situation and tried to lighten the mood with an awkward “Haha”, his cheeks twitching as he searched for something to say. But Chiharu's expression did not waver in the slightest. Gradually, his face grew pale, and sweat began to gather on his skin.

The classroom fell silent. No one had made any particularly loud noise, yet an oppressive tension filled the room.

Even Yukino, watching the situation unfold, could hear the sound of herself swallowing far too loudly.

Sympathy.

Only Konatsu Sayama knew how dangerous it was to apply that word to the relationship between Chiharu and Yukino as it stood now. One might say Ota had been unlucky.

But generally speaking, a third party arbitrarily deciding that a friendship between two people was based on sympathy would be considered wrong.

Chiharu suddenly wondered why she hadn't been angry when Konatsu had made the same accusation.

Perhaps, then, Konatsu's words, coming from someone who knew the circumstances, had hit a little closer to the truth than she wanted to admit.

And that was precisely why she couldn't forgive Ota. He knew nothing of the complicated background between them. Without thinking, he had casually dismissed the feelings she had built up over time, judging only by what he could see on the surface. Her anger flared anew.

But just as Chiharu was about to continue, someone pinched the fabric of her blouse around her waist.

Chiharu turned around, as though jolted back to her senses.

Yukino was looking at her with a worried, bewildered expression. Behind her slightly long, dark-brown fringe, her pomegranate-red gemstone eyes trembled with unease.

Seeing that, Chiharu relaxed her shoulders and let out a small sigh.

Still angry at the insult to her relationship with Yukino, but also dismayed at herself for having let her emotions get the better of her, she turned back towards Ota and left him with one final, emotionless remark.

“What you just said was rude. You shouldn't say things like that to other people.”
