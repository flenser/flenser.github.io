I'm sorry, I'll be a little late.

It was just as Chiharu received that message from Yukino.

Standing at the spot where she had parted ways with Konatsu, Chiharu was fiddling with her phone. She furrowed her brow slightly as she considered how to reply, then looked around. That was when she spotted a woman who looked deathly pale.

The woman was running down the festival's main street, frantically calling out someone's name. She was attracting quite a bit of attention.

She looked to be in her thirties. Chiharu guessed that she had become separated from her child.

She had no clear idea what she could do to help, but before she knew it, she had sent Yukino a quick message saying, I might be late, too, then immediately stood up and hurried over.

“Um… are you looking for someone?”

The woman was gasping for breath, her face pale as a sheet. “M-my daughter,” she murmured.

After managing to steady her breathing, she looked around frantically and spoke in an anxious voice.

“I've become separated from my daughter… I've looked everywhere, but I can't find her.”

Just as Chiharu had expected. Without another moment's hesitation, she took it upon herself to help search for the girl, as naturally as if it were the most obvious thing in the world.

They needed to act quickly, but there was no need to panic. Chiharu held out both hands in a calming gesture as she said, “I'll help you look.” The woman nodded desperately several times, expressing her gratitude with her eyes.

“Have you checked the place where you got separated?”

“Y-yes. I asked the shopkeepers, too, but none of them had seen her…”\
“Then there's a good chance she's walking around looking for you. That's actually reassuring.”

Chiharu offered this hypothesis, mixing in a little convenient reassurance to calm the mother, who looked on the verge of losing control.

“In other words, your daughter may be asking the adults around her for help finding you. If that happens, I'm sure she'll find someone kind who'll help her. And that brings us to the lost children's centre. At festivals on this scale, there's almost always one set up at the main office or information desk in case something happens. How about we head there, keeping an eye out for your daughter along the way?”

Chiharu could feel just how desperately the mother was worrying that her child might be in danger. Her own concern was stirred by it, but as an outsider, she was also able to think calmly. That was why she made the suggestion.

The mother nodded repeatedly, her face on the verge of tears.

“Yes…”

The two of them set off at a brisk pace, pushing through the crowds of people cheerfully weaving between the food stalls, their urgency at odds with the festive atmosphere around them.

They decided to look for the lost children's centre by following the signs posted nearby.

There had been staff wearing fluorescent vests around the station, but the main street was so crowded that they could not find any. If they had managed to locate one, they might have been able to confirm the situation over the radio, but there was nothing to be done about that.

The two women hurried along the street, but the mother seemed to have difficulty running in her high heels.

She looked ready to rip them off in frustration, but Chiharu stopped her, urging her to stay calm.

“If your daughter is already at the lost children's centre, rushing around will only put you at risk of injuring yourself. Let's take it easy. Besides, just in case, I'll search the surrounding area as we go. I'm wearing trainers, after all.”

Chiharu pointed down at her feet. The mother thanked her in a tearful voice.

“Thank you so much.”\
“Could you tell me your daughter's name, what she's wearing, and where you became separated?”

The mother narrowed her eyes as she looked into space, recalling the details as best she could.

Chiharu made a note of everything on her phone. For the moment, she put aside the fact that Yukino had not replied, nodded once and said, “All right, I've got it.”

Finally, they each wrote their phone number on a piece of paper, tore the sheets in half and exchanged contact details. Then, without turning away from the direction of the lost children's centre, they set off on a somewhat winding search through the sea of people, trying to find a few grains of gold in all that sand.

It was hot. The heat was stifling. Sweat was pouring off her. Her breathing was becoming ragged. She had been outdoors for a long time already, and her feet were tired.

Lanterns filled her field of vision. Their warm orange glow seemed to make her body feel even hotter.

Chiharu could feel the sweat running down her cheeks and her breathing growing laboured, but she never stopped. She went from stall to stall, asking the people working there, “We're looking for a lost child.”

She knew she was inconveniencing them. But even if they found her irritating, she would see it through.

She had always been like this. Perhaps it was a form of the just-world hypothesis.

It might also have something in common with the idea that people should be rewarded or punished according to their actions. In short, she wanted people who worked hard and did the right thing to be rewarded, and she could not stand seeing good people get the short end of the stick. Conversely, she found it equally intolerable when bad people were the ones who prospered.

That did not mean she wanted to suggest that victims were somehow at fault. She simply wanted to believe in the convenient parts of the world, and perhaps that was nothing more than idealism.

So what? In the end, it was just self-satisfaction. If someone had done nothing wrong and was nevertheless in trouble, then she wanted to help them.

I want to live in a way that makes someone happy, so that I can face my reflection tomorrow with my head held high.

“I saw her.”

A burly, intimidating-looking man running a shooting gallery, with a towel wrapped around his head, rubbed his chin stubble and spoke apologetically.

“Sorry. I was busy, so I didn't have a chance to call out to her. But about ten minutes ago, I think? She walked off in that direction.”

The man pointed towards the lost children's centre, the same direction Chiharu and the mother were heading.

Relieved to have confirmation that they were going the right way, Chiharu breathed a sigh of relief.

“That's wonderful. Thank you so much!”

She thanked the man, then hurried after the mother.

“Apparently she was heading this way about ten minutes ago!”

Hearing this, the mother realised that her daughter had at least been moving about of her own accord. She breathed a sigh of relief and quickened her pace, her face still close to tears.

“Were you out with your mummy today? That sounds lovely.”

They were on their way to the lost children's centre, and had covered about seventy per cent of the distance.

Yukino was carrying the little girl on her back, barely able to catch her breath, but she kept talking to her so that she would not become anxious.

“Mm-hm,” the girl replied, clinging to Yukino. Then she added, “You know what?”

She seemed to have become a little more talkative.

“It's my birthday today.”

Yukino and the older woman exchanged glances.

“Happy birthday!”\
“Happy birthday, sweetheart.”

The girl nodded, resting her chin against Yukino's back. Then her expression clouded slightly.

“So Mummy said we'd go to the festival together. But Mummy's always busy, you know. So… I wanted to buy her something with my pocket money, and I wandered away from her.”

The girl's voice gradually became muffled, taking on a damp, tearful quality.

Yukino and the woman exchanged glances, understanding how the girl had become separated from her mother. But it was not their place to pass judgement on that. And certainly, there was no reason to scold a little girl who was already feeling anxious.

Yukino, barely able to breathe, forced a smile and squeezed out a voice that was as bright and cheerful as she could manage.

“That's so lovely! What a wonderful thing to do!”

“…Really?” the girl asked in a voice that sounded on the verge of tears, tightening her arms around Yukino.

“Really,” Yukino assured her.

“I think she'd be absolutely delighted. So when you see your mummy, tell her about it, and then the two of you can talk about what to buy together. I'm sure that would make her even happier.”

Yukino did not want to make promises too casually, but the very fact that this girl cared enough about her mother to want to do something for her meant that she must have a wonderful mother.

It was just then that the older woman walking with them touched her earpiece and began exchanging a few words with someone, her expression turning serious. After several back-and-forth exchanges, her face finally relaxed.

The woman noticed the two of them watching her. She smiled warmly and pointed ahead, indicating that they should keep going.

“Your mummy's been found. You'll be able to see her at the lost children's centre.”
